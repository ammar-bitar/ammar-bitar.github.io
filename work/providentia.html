Seeing the Autobahn through an event camera

Vehicle detection from cameras on motorway gantries, using an event camera alongside a frame camera, with a focus on night and heavy rain.

Where
Intel Labs, Munich, 2020 to 2022, within the Providentia++ motorway-sensing programme
Scope
Event-camera vehicle detection, DVS-to-RGB co-calibration and sensor fusion, YOLO fine-tuned for night and heavy precipitation, benchmarked against the RGB and CNN baseline
Setting
Cameras on the gantries that carry the overhead signs, looking down the Autobahn
Sensors
A dynamic vision sensor (DVS) event camera working alongside a conventional RGB camera
About the figures
The diagrams and interactive pieces here are built for this page: working simulations of the ideas, written from scratch so you can play with them.

01Why an event camera on a gantry

Providentia++ put sensors on the gantries along a stretch of motorway so that vehicles could be detected and tracked from the infrastructure instead of from the car. Frame cameras on a gantry struggle at night and in rain: exposure times get longer, headlights bloom, and rain adds streaks and glare. Detectors trained on daytime images get worse in these conditions.

An event camera does not take frames. Each pixel reports on its own when the brightness it sees changes by more than a threshold, and whether it went up (an ON event) or down (an OFF event). Nothing is reported where nothing changes. With a fixed camera over a road, the static road produces no events and each moving vehicle produces events along its edges, with microsecond timing and a much higher dynamic range than a frame sensor. The project tested whether that advantage holds up with a real detector, in bad weather, and alongside an RGB camera.

02The build

  • Event-based vehicle detection. Event streams were accumulated into representations a detector can consume (see below), and YOLO models were fine-tuned on them, with the fine-tuning aimed specifically at robustness at night and in heavy precipitation.
  • DVS-to-RGB co-calibration. The two cameras see the same road from slightly different positions and with different optics. Before their outputs can be combined, every event pixel has to map onto a known position in the frame image. I built the co-calibration between the event camera and the RGB camera so that detections from one could be placed in the other's coordinates.
  • Sensor fusion. With the cameras calibrated, detections from the event and frame streams were fused. The frame camera contributes appearance and colour in good light; the event camera contributes motion edges when the light is poor.
  • Benchmarking. The event-based pipeline was evaluated against an RGB-and-CNN baseline, with attention to the night and rain conditions the programme cared about.

03Turning events into something a detector can read

A raw event is a four-tuple: x, y, timestamp, polarity. A detector like YOLO wants a tensor. How you get from one to the other decides what the detector can see, so a good part of the work was building and comparing event-frame representations. Three families, next to the frame they come from:

  • Binning. Count or sum events per pixel over a fixed window, optionally with ON and OFF in separate channels. Cheap and dense, but everything that happened in the window lands on one plane, so a fast vehicle smears.
  • Voxel grids, 3D and 4D. Split the window into B time bins and accumulate into a B × H × W tensor, with each event's weight spread linearly between its two nearest bins. Keeping polarity as a separate channel gives a 4D tensor. Order of motion survives, at the cost of memory and a heavier input.
  • Leaky binning. Instead of hard windows, integrate with a decay: each pixel keeps its recent events and forgets older ones on a time constant, similar to a time surface. This helps because a vehicle at 130 km/h and one crawling in a jam produce very different event rates.

Which representation works best depends on traffic speed, lighting and the detector, so comparing them was part of the work.

04What it taught me

Most of the effort went into the parts around the detector. The event representation made a real difference, so we compared several of them instead of picking one up front. The event camera and the RGB camera had to be calibrated against each other before fusing their detections made any sense. And the tests that counted were the ones at night and in rain, since that is where the event camera was supposed to help.
