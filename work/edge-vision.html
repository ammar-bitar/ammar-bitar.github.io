Ammar Bitar / Work / Case study

Catching defects at line speed

Two production lines with very different inspection problems: a robot cell sorting solar panels, and a 200 m paste line where the material changes as it hardens.

Where
Siemens AG, Vienna, 2023 to 2025, across customer projects
Scope
Detection, segmentation and anomaly detection on industrial PCs and Nvidia Jetson, HMI and robot integration, and the related foundation-model research
Lines
Automated quality inspection in solar-panel manufacturing, with a robotic pick and place; and multi-sensor anomaly detection along a 200 m paste line with four camera stations
Stack
PyTorch, TensorRT, ONNX, DeepStream and GStreamer in C++, Triton, Jetson Linux, Docker, Siemens HMI
About the figures
The diagrams and interactive pieces here are built for this page: working simulations of the ideas, written from scratch so you can play with them.

01What a line demands

Product moves past the camera at a fixed rate, and the decision has to be made before the piece reaches whatever acts on it downstream. That rules out sending images to a server, so the model runs next to the line, on an industrial PC or a Jetson.

Defects are also rare. A line that runs well produces very few, so the class you care about has the fewest examples, and false alarms quickly cost an operator's trust. The result also has to go somewhere useful: in practice, an HMI for the operator and a machine that acts on it.

02Solar panels: from a score to a robot arm

Panels arrive on the conveyor, the camera inspects each one, and a model segments and scores it for defects such as cracks, cell damage, soldering faults and contamination. The verdict goes to a Siemens HMI, where the operator sees the panel, the defect map and the decision, and to a robot cell, which picks the panel off the belt and places it in the OK or the NOK position.

Much of the work was in closing that loop: triggering the camera from the line so each image captures a whole panel, the handshake with the PLC so the robot makes the right pick, and an HMI view the operator can read quickly. A missed defect means a faulty panel ships; a false reject means a good panel is scrapped, so the threshold has to be easy for the plant to adjust.

03A paste that changes as it travels

The other line was 200 m long with four camera stations, inspecting a paste that is applied wet at the start of the line and is hard by the end. At the first station it is glossy and still flowing, and the typical defects are bubbles, thin spots and edge run-out. By the last station it is matt and firm, and the defects are cracks, flaking and surface breaks. Some early defects disappear as the paste sets, and some defects seen at the end started much earlier on the line.

Each station therefore has its own model and thresholds, trained on what the paste looks like at that point on the line.

04Getting a model into a cabinet

The pipeline runs in C++ through DeepStream and GStreamer, so frames go from the camera to the network without going through Python. The network is exported to ONNX and compiled to a TensorRT engine for the specific Jetson it runs on, with the precision chosen to meet the latency budget. Where several models share a device, Triton serves them. Everything is packaged in containers so units can be updated consistently.

05Foundation models for a problem with few examples

Every new product, and on the paste line every station, means a new inspection task, and a line that runs well produces few defects to learn from. Training a detector from scratch each time is slow and needs exactly the images that are missing. So in a small research team I co-led our work on using foundation models here: start from a model that already knows what object boundaries look like and adapt it with the few examples the line provides. In practice that meant fine-tuning SAM with LoRA, which trains a small set of extra weights and leaves the backbone unchanged. A new inspection task then becomes a fine-tuning run on a handful of examples, not a new labelling campaign.

The other approach was to generate examples. I researched diffusion models for creating synthetic defects on real product images, to cover failure modes that rarely occur on the line.

The training ran on a cluster that I built and debugged with one other research engineer: 16 H100 GPUs on a multi-node Slurm cluster, DDP for data parallelism and DeepSpeed to shard model states, working through NCCL, throughput, out-of-memory and checkpointing issues, including a period on Ethernet before InfiniBand was available.

06What it taught me

A model that does well offline still has to hold up on the line, where lighting, camera position and the product itself vary more than in any test set. Most of my time went into the work around the model: triggering, the handover to the HMI and the robot, setting thresholds the plant could work with, and getting the hard cases back into training. The paste line added something I had not dealt with before. The material changes along the line, so each station needs its own idea of what a defect looks like.
